\paragraph{Key Findings for RQ1:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item All devices show a measurable power increase when ports are loaded, ranging from +222\,mW (Alcatel, 2~ports) to +707\,mW (Asus, 4~ports).
    \item The \textbf{Fritzbox~7530} exhibits a non-monotonic power profile: power peaks at 2~ports (5055\,mW) and then \emph{decreases} at 3~ports (5040\,mW). This is due to CPU saturation---the device cannot sustain full throughput on 3+ ports, so it does less total work and consumes slightly less power. This is a noteworthy finding: under CPU-bound conditions, adding more loaded ports can actually \emph{reduce} total power consumption because the bottleneck shifts from the PHY/switching fabric to the CPU.

    \item The per-port power increment is small relative to idle power: approximately 30--175\,mW per additional loaded port, depending on the device.
\end{itemize}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Measurement Considerations}
\label{sec:measurement-considerations}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

Several factors should be considered when interpreting these results:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Power meter resolution:} The DECT~200 reports power in discrete steps of approximately 70\,mW. For devices where the load-induced power increase is small (e.g., Huawei: $\sim$550\,mW over 4~ports), the per-port incremental power ($\sim$137\,mW) is only about 2 discrete steps, limiting measurement precision for fine-grained per-port analysis.
    \item \textbf{CPU-targeted traffic:} All traffic was directed at the device's own IP, engaging the CPU stack rather than the hardware switching fabric. A Layer~2 forwarding test (e.g., port A$\rightarrow$B loopback) would likely show different power characteristics.
    \item \textbf{No zero-cable baseline:} The pre-test baseline had all cables physically connected (link up, no traffic). The power cost of merely maintaining a GbE link (PHY active, auto-negotiation) vs.\ having no cable connected was not isolated. \emph{Test~3 (Phase~0) is designed to provide this missing zero-cable measurement.}
    \item \textbf{Throughput anomalies:} Some data points show transient throughput spikes (especially the Fritzbox and Alcatel) that exceed steady-state values. These are measurement artifacts caused by buffered packet bursts aligning with the 5-second polling window.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Results: Throughput Scaling (Single Port)}
\label{sec:results-throughput-scaling}

This section presents the results of Test~2 (Section~\ref{sec:test-throughput-scaling}), which measures how power consumption scales with single-port throughput using a four-step ramp profile.
Three GbE devices (Fritzbox~7530, Huawei~EG8245W5, Asus~RT-AX68U) were tested with a 1\,Gbps link at target rates of 250, 500, 750, and 1000\,Mbps.

Only a \emph{single} Ethernet cable was connected during this test, enabling a direct comparison with Test~1's four-cable idle power to quantify the per-port overhead (\textbf{RQ2}).

\subsection{Summary: RQ2 and RQ3}
\label{sec:test2-rq-answers}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{RQ2: Per-port idle power overhead.}
Each idle GbE connection adds 120--395\,mW to the device's power consumption, depending on the device.
For devices with higher per-port overhead (Huawei, Asus), the cost of three idle cables ($\sim$1.0--1.2\,W) exceeds the power increase from driving a single port at full 1\,Gbps throughput ($\sim$0.4\,W).
This finding suggests that \textbf{disconnecting unused Ethernet cables offers a measurable---though modest---power saving}, especially for devices with high per-port overhead.

\paragraph{RQ3: Power vs.\ throughput scaling.}
Power consumption scales \emph{sub-linearly} with network throughput for all tested devices.
The total idle-to-full-load increase ranges from 120\,mW (Alcatel, 100\,Mbps) to 444\,mW (Asus, 1\,Gbps)---only 4.9--7.1\% of idle power.
This means that \textbf{the base platform power dominates}, and the marginal cost of actual data transmission is small.
For energy-saving purposes, reducing idle power (e.g., through device selection or power-saving modes) will have far greater impact than limiting throughput.

\paragraph{Fritzbox eco mode is the most effective.}
The Fritzbox ``Green Mode'' reduces power across \emph{all} subsystems, not just WiFi.
The Ethernet idle cost drops from 3\,041\,mW to 2\,475\,mW ($-$566\,mW, $-$18.6\%).
This confirms that eco mode forces the Ethernet PHY to a lower power state---consistent with the observation that under full Ethernet load in eco mode, each port achieved only $\sim$95\,Mbps instead of $\sim$460\,Mbps, suggesting the PHY was downshifted to 100\,Mbps operation.
At full combined load, eco mode saves 474\,mW ($-$7.0\%).
Annual savings at continuous operation: $\sim$4.2\,kWh or $\sim$\euro{1.04} at \euro{0.25}/kWh.

\paragraph{Asus TX power reduction has minimal impact.}
Setting both radios to the lowest TX power saves only 116--149\,mW at idle and 356\,mW under WiFi load ($-$3.1\%).
Under combined load the saving is negligible ($-$35\,mW, $-$0.3\%).
WiFi throughput actually \emph{increased} from 147\,Mbps (Run~A) to 163--192\,Mbps (Run~B), likely due to reduced interference or environmental variation between runs rather than a causal relationship with TX power.

\paragraph{Huawei TX power reduction shows anomalous behavior.}
While idle WiFi power decreased modestly ($-$163\,mW at 2.4\,GHz, $-$63\,mW at 5\,GHz), the WiFi load and combined load phases showed a \emph{large increase} of +2\,146\,mW and +2\,172\,mW respectively in Run~B.
WiFi throughput also increased from 58.5\,Mbps (Run~A) to 98.3\,Mbps (Run~B).
This counter-intuitive result may be due to the Huawei's Wi-Fi-to-Wi-Fi test topology (two phones communicating through the router): the reduced TX power setting may have triggered different modulation or retry behavior, or the phones adapted their own TX power, resulting in higher overall power consumption at the router.
This result should be treated with caution and does not necessarily generalize to typical usage.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (Per-port PHY cost):} Connecting an idle GbE cable costs 265--302\,mW on average; Fast Ethernet (Alcatel) costs $\sim$172\,mW. The first cable can cost more due to PHY initialization overhead.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Distance and Band Analysis}
\label{sec:results-wifi-distance-band}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsubsection{Effect of Distance on Power}

Contrary to initial expectations, moving the client from the near to the wall position does \emph{not} consistently increase the router's power consumption.
For some devices, the wall position actually shows \emph{lower} power draw during load, because reduced signal quality leads to lower effective throughput, and consequently less data-processing work for the router.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Asus 5\,GHz:} Power overhead \emph{increases} from 2\,992\,mW (near) to 3\,751\,mW (wall, +25\%), despite throughput dropping from 584 to 244\,Mbps. This suggests the Asus boosts transmit power at greater distance, partially compensating for path loss.
    \item \textbf{Asus 2.4\,GHz:} Power overhead \emph{drops sharply} from 3\,101\,mW (near) to only 996\,mW (wall, $-$68\%), with throughput falling from 143 to 33\,Mbps. At this low data rate, the radio is mostly idle between frame bursts.
    \item \textbf{Fritzbox:} The 5\,GHz overhead increases slightly (788$\to$1\,023\,mW), while 2.4\,GHz overhead decreases (525$\to$373\,mW). Both changes correlate with the throughput reduction.
    \item \textbf{Huawei:} Both bands show slightly higher overhead at the wall position, consistent with increased retransmissions (743~retransmits at near vs.\ 836 at wall for 5\,GHz).
    \item \textbf{Alcatel:} The wall position draws 372\,mW more than near (+47\%), despite only a small throughput reduction (94$\to$88\,Mbps). This may reflect increased error correction and retransmission overhead.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Effect of Frequency Band}

Comparing 2.4\,GHz vs.\ 5\,GHz at the same distance reveals significant differences in both throughput and power:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{5\,GHz achieves much higher throughput at near distance,} with the Asus reaching 584\,Mbps (5\,GHz) vs.\ 143\,Mbps (2.4\,GHz), and the Fritzbox 235 vs.\ 89\,Mbps. This is primarily due to wider channel bandwidths (the Asus uses 160\,MHz on 5\,GHz vs.\ 40\,MHz on 2.4\,GHz).
    \item \textbf{5\,GHz suffers more from wall attenuation:} The Asus 5\,GHz throughput drops 58\% through the wall (584$\to$244\,Mbps), while 2.4\,GHz drops 77\% (143$\to$33\,Mbps). However, the Fritzbox 5\,GHz drops 74\% (235$\to$61\,Mbps) while its 2.4\,GHz drops only 43\% (89$\to$51\,Mbps), confirming the general principle that lower frequencies penetrate walls better.
    \item \textbf{Power overhead:} On the Asus, the 5\,GHz overhead ($\sim$3.0--3.8\,W) is comparable to 2.4\,GHz at near distance (3.1\,W) but much higher at wall distance (3.8 vs.\ 1.0\,W). The Fritzbox shows more modest overhead on both bands (0.4--1.0\,W).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Huawei: Unusually High Variability}

The Huawei exhibited extremely variable throughput during Wi-Fi tests, with per-second bitrates swinging between 0 and 253\,Mbps in the 5\,GHz near test (standard deviation: 61\,Mbps over the 3-minute load phase).
The 2.4\,GHz band fared even worse, averaging only 32\,Mbps at the near position with similarly erratic behaviour.
This is accompanied by a high number of TCP retransmissions (743 at 5\,GHz near, 836 at 5\,GHz wall, 68 at 2.4\,GHz near).
The root cause is likely the test topology: because the Huawei isolates LAN and Wi-Fi clients, iperf3 ran between two Wi-Fi devices through the router's Wi-Fi bridge, meaning \emph{both} the sending and receiving path traverse the wireless medium.
This doubles the impact of wireless congestion and interference, explaining the much lower and more erratic throughput compared to the other routers where iperf3 ran between a Wi-Fi client and a wired PC.

\subsubsection{Alcatel: LAN Bottleneck and ``Both Wi-Fi'' Tests}

The standard Alcatel tests route Wi-Fi traffic through the 100\,Mbps Ethernet LAN port to the test PC.
Since the Alcatel's Wi-Fi achieved 94\,Mbps near and 88\,Mbps at the wall position---very close to the 100\,Mbps limit---the LAN port may be the throughput bottleneck rather than Wi-Fi.

To test this, additional runs placed the test PC on Wi-Fi as well (``both Wi-Fi'').
Results show throughput of 86\,Mbps (near) and 64\,Mbps (wall), \emph{lower} than the LAN-connected tests.
This indicates that the 100\,Mbps LAN port was \emph{not} the bottleneck: the Alcatel's 802.11n radio simply cannot sustain more than $\sim$94\,Mbps under favourable conditions.
In the ``both Wi-Fi'' configuration, the additional contention from two clients sharing the same radio further reduced throughput, particularly at the wall position.

Interestingly, the ``both Wi-Fi'' tests show \emph{lower} power overhead (623\,mW near, 766\,mW wall) compared to the standard tests (799\,mW, 1\,171\,mW), which is consistent with the lower throughput reducing the router's processing load.

\subsection{RQ5: Comprehensive Wi-Fi Power Analysis}
\label{sec:results-wifi-rq5}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

Test~4 provides a comprehensive answer to RQ5, which asks about the impact of frequency band, signal strength, and client distance on Wi-Fi power consumption.

\paragraph{Key findings:}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Wi-Fi causes substantially higher power overhead than wired Ethernet.}
    Comparing with Test~2 (wired throughput scaling), where the Asus drew only 444\,mW extra at 1\,Gbps wired throughput, the same router draws 3.0--3.8\,W extra for Wi-Fi at 244--584\,Mbps---roughly \textbf{7--8$\times$ more power per unit of throughput}. The Fritzbox similarly shows 525--1\,023\,mW for Wi-Fi vs.\ 282\,mW for wired 1\,Gbps. This is expected, as Wi-Fi transmission involves analogue RF circuits, beamforming, and protocol overhead that wired Ethernet does not require.

    \item \textbf{Distance has a mixed effect on power.}
    Moving the client behind a wall does \emph{not} uniformly increase router power. For the Asus at 2.4\,GHz, wall placement \emph{reduced} power overhead by 68\% because throughput dropped proportionally. At 5\,GHz, the Asus showed increased power at the wall position despite lower throughput, suggesting active transmit-power boosting. The effect is device- and band-dependent, ruling out a simple ``more distance = more power'' conclusion.

    \item \textbf{5\,GHz is more energy-efficient than 2.4\,GHz (when signal is strong).}
    At the near position, 5\,GHz achieves 2--4$\times$ higher throughput than 2.4\,GHz for similar power overhead, yielding much better energy efficiency (Asus: 66 vs.\ 17\,Mbps/W; Fritzbox: 54 vs.\ 22\,Mbps/W). However, 5\,GHz efficiency degrades more sharply through walls, and in the worst case (Huawei 5\,GHz wall: 4.4\,Mbps/W) it becomes less efficient than 2.4\,GHz in good conditions.

    \item \textbf{Channel width is the dominant factor for throughput.}
    The Asus, which uses 160\,MHz channels on 5\,GHz, achieves 584\,Mbps---2.5$\times$ more than the Fritzbox (235\,Mbps on 80\,MHz) and 7$\times$ more than the Huawei (83\,Mbps). This underscores that \emph{channel configuration} matters more than the mere choice of frequency band.

    \item \textbf{Absolute power draw varies enormously between devices.}
    At comparable workloads (2.4\,GHz near), the Alcatel draws only 2.6\,W total, the Fritzbox 4.1\,W, the Asus 8.6\,W, and the Huawei 8.7\,W. The cheapest and simplest device is also the most power-efficient in absolute terms, reinforcing the Test~1 finding that platform power dominates.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Throughput Measurement Anomaly}
\label{sec:test5b-throughput-anomaly}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

The raw throughput data from Configuration~B exhibited a significant measurement artifact.
While the first loaded interface (Ethernet, i.e.\ port~1) consistently reported the expected $\sim$955\,Mbps, all subsequently activated interfaces reported dramatically inflated values:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{2-port phase:} Ethernet~5 reported $\sim$32\,841\,Mbps (raw) instead of the expected $\sim$955\,Mbps.
    \item \textbf{3-port phase:} Ethernet~5 and~6 each reported $\sim$18\,500\,Mbps.
    \item \textbf{4-port phase:} Ethernet~5, 6, and~7 each reported $\sim$12\,100\,Mbps.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Notably, the \emph{aggregate} inflated throughput remained roughly constant at $\sim$33\,000--38\,000\,Mbps regardless of how many ports were active, and the inflated total was shared equally among the affected interfaces.
Port~1 (Ethernet) always reported correct values ($\sim$955\,Mbps) throughout all phases.

\paragraph{Root cause analysis.}
The pattern is consistent with a load generator reporting \emph{sent} bytes rather than \emph{acknowledged/delivered} bytes.
In this topology, the Asus router's WAN uplink to the Huawei bridge is a single Gigabit Ethernet connection, limiting total forwarded throughput to $\sim$955\,Mbps regardless of how many LAN ports are loaded.
The load generator appears to count all bytes written to the network socket---including packets that are buffered in the NIC or dropped by the switch fabric---as ``throughput.''
Since the Realtek 2.5\,GbE NIC can accept data from the application layer faster than the 1\,Gbps WAN link can drain it, the excess is counted but never delivered.

The constant aggregate ($\sim$35\,Gbps total) likely reflects the combined DMA throughput ceiling of the Realtek~RTL8125 controller, which is shared across all virtual queues.
When one port is inflated, it uses the full budget; when four ports are inflated, they each get one-quarter.

\paragraph{Correction approach.}
Since the theoretical maximum per Gigabit Ethernet interface is $\sim$1\,000\,Mbps, all per-interface throughput values are capped at 1\,000\,Mbps for analysis.
The filtered throughput column in Table~\ref{tab:test5b_stats} reflects this correction.
\textbf{Power measurements are unaffected} by this artifact, as the DECT~200 measures actual electrical power independently.

\subsubsection{Analysis}

The results clearly show that adding a dedicated router to a bridge-mode ISP device \emph{increases} total system power consumption substantially:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Idle overhead:} The two-device setup draws 16\,632\,mW at idle, which is 7\,670\,mW (+85.6\%) more than the Huawei alone (8\,962\,mW). This overhead is almost entirely accounted for by the Asus's idle power of 8\,226\,mW (from Test~1), with a small offset suggesting the Huawei consumes slightly less in bridge mode ($16\,632 - 8\,226 = 8\,406$\,mW) than in router mode (8\,962\,mW)---a reduction of $\sim$556\,mW or 6.2\%.

    \item \textbf{Load scaling:} Under full 4-port load, the combined system draws 17\,072\,mW vs.\ 9\,510\,mW standalone---an overhead of 7\,562\,mW. The overhead decreases slightly with load (from +85.6\% at idle to +79.5\% at 4~ports), because the Huawei in bridge mode does not need to perform NAT/firewall processing and thus its load-dependent power increase is smaller.

    \item \textbf{Bridge mode power savings on the Huawei:} By subtracting the Asus's Test~1 power values from the combined measurement, we can estimate the Huawei's power in bridge mode:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Idle: $16\,632 - 8\,226 = 8\,406$\,mW (vs.\ 8\,962\,mW in router mode: $-$556\,mW)
        \item 4-port: $17\,072 - 8\,933 = 8\,139$\,mW (vs.\ 9\,510\,mW in router mode: $-$1\,371\,mW)
    \end{itemize}
    Bridge mode appears to reduce the Huawei's power consumption by 556--1\,371\,mW (6--14\%), confirming that disabling NAT, DHCP, and firewall does save measurable power on the ISP device.

    \item \textbf{Net effect:} Despite the Huawei's power savings in bridge mode, the Asus router's idle draw of 8\,226\,mW far outweighs the 556\,mW saved. The net system cost is approximately 7.1--7.7\,W higher in the bridge configuration. This makes the bridge-mode split \emph{energetically unfavorable} unless the features provided by the dedicated router (advanced QoS, VPN, NAS, better Wi-Fi) justify the additional power budget.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{RQ8: Is Bridge Mode More Energy Efficient?}

\textbf{No.} The bridge-mode configuration consumes approximately 80--86\% more power than the standalone Huawei at every load level.
While bridge mode does reduce the ISP device's own power consumption by 6--14\%, this saving is dwarfed by the dedicated router's idle power draw ($\sim$8.2\,W).

The decision to use bridge mode should therefore be driven by \emph{functional} requirements (e.g., needing features the ISP device does not provide) rather than energy considerations.
Users who do deploy a bridge-mode setup should be aware that the additional router adds a persistent $\sim$8\,W to their home network's baseline power consumption, corresponding to approximately 70\,kWh per year ($8\,\text{W} \times 8\,760\,\text{h}$) or roughly €25 at typical European electricity rates.
